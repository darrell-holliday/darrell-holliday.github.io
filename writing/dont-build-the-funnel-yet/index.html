Don’t build a funnel until someone has actually bought

I hear a version of this every week. “I’m building the funnel first, then I’ll start talking to people.”

The landing page is almost done. The email sequence is mapped. The booking calendar is connected to a tool they set up at 1 a.m. And they have not asked a single person to buy.

A funnel is a way to repeat a sale you already understand. If you do not yet know why someone would say yes, you are decorating a guess.

Here’s the order I use, and the one I ask clients to use.

Write the offer in plain words. Who it’s for, what changes, why now, and what you want them to do next. If you cannot say that in a short paragraph, you are not ready for a page, let alone a six-step sequence.

Make a short list of people you can already reach. Not “my audience.” Names. People who have already talked to you, already asked a question, or already work in the world you want to serve.

Talk to them. A call, a voice note, a direct message that is actually a conversation. Tell them who the offer is for and what it does. Ask if they want to do it. When they hesitate, write down the hesitation. That sentence is more useful than any headline generator.

Get a yes, or a clear no, before you build the machine. A yes tells you what to keep. A no tells you what to fix. A silent landing page tells you nothing.

Then, and only then, build the smallest path that would have made that sale easier: a simple page, a follow-up message you can reuse, a way to book the next call. That is a funnel. It is just the conversation you already had, written down.

I’ve watched people spend months on tools and pages for an offer nobody had agreed to pay for. I’ve also watched first sales happen from a Google Doc and a calendar link, because the offer was clear and the person asked.

If you have been “finishing the funnel” for three weeks, stop this week. Pick five people. Have the conversation. Build the funnel around what you learn, not around what you hope.
