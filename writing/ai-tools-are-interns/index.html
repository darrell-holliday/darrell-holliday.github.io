Writing

Treat your AI tools like interns, not employees

AI is fast and cheap and it will confidently do the wrong thing. Here’s the standard I hold it to.

I like AI. I use it every week. I also don’t let it talk to a buyer until I’ve read what it wrote.

The simplest way to think about it: your AI tools are interns.

Interns are useful. They draft, they research, they organize. They also invent details, miss the point, and will hit send if you let them. You wouldn’t put an intern on a sales call alone in week one. Don’t do that with a chatbot either.

Here’s the standard I use with clients.

Interns draft. You decide. Let it write the first version of the offer, the follow-up, the outline. Then you cut the fluff and fix anything you wouldn’t say out loud.

Interns need a tight brief. “Write my sales page” is how you get sludge. “Here’s who it’s for, here’s the before and after, don’t use these words” is how you get something you can edit.

Interns don’t own the relationship. If a person is waiting on a reply, you send it. Automation can remind you. It shouldn’t pretend to be you.

Fire the intern from any job that’s failing. If the output is consistently off, the prompt is wrong, or the job shouldn’t be automated yet. Go back to doing it by hand for a week. You’ll learn more than you will by adding another tool.

The people who get the most out of AI aren’t the ones with the biggest stack. They’re the ones who kept a human in the loop, especially where trust is the product.

Use the intern. Don’t give it the keys.
